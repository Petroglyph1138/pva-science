Fusion in the core

Drag hydrogen atoms (H) into the glowing fusion zone. When four hydrogen atoms join, they make one helium atom (He), which stays in the core.
Helium made: 0 · Energy bursts: 0
Think: Why does it take more than one hydrogen atom to make one helium atom? Where does a star's light and heat come from? (Key Fact 3)
In real stars, four hydrogen nuclei end up as one helium nucleus. The reading's picture shows two to keep it simple. Each joining really does give off a tiny burst of energy, but a real core has trillions of them every second, so it shines steadily instead of flashing. The flash here shows one joining up close. The new helium stays in the core and slowly builds up there, just as it does in the Sun.

Why do stars twinkle?

Starlight has to pass through Earth's air before it reaches your eyes. The air is always moving, and moving air bends the light a little this way and that. A star is so far away that its light comes from one tiny point, so each bend makes it flicker. The more stirred up the air, the harder and faster a star twinkles.
Think: Watch both sides at the same time. What happens on the right each time the light bends on the left? Move the slider from calm to windy. How does the twinkling change, and why?

Star or planet?

The star's light comes from one tiny point, so moving air makes it flicker. The planet's light comes from a tiny disk, so it stays steady.
Think: Move the slider from calm to windy. Which one twinkles more as the air gets rougher? How did Noor's cousin know the steady dot was a planet?

Near and far

Giant: 1× as far as the Sun-like star
Close up, the giant star looks much brighter.
Think: The giant is 100 times wider than the Sun-like star. Why can it look dimmer? Why does our Sun look bigger and brighter than every other star? (Key Fact 1)
Model: both stars have the same surface temperature, so the giant gives off 10,000 times as much light. Brightness drops with the square of the distance, as real starlight does, so the two match at 100 times as far.
